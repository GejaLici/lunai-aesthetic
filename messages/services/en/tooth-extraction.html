Reasons for Tooth Extraction

While our primary goal is always to save your natural teeth, an extraction may be the healthiest option in several scenarios:

  • Severe Decay: When a cavity has destroyed too much tooth structure to support a filling or crown.
  • Advanced Gum Disease: When bone loss is so significant that the tooth is loose and cannot be stabilized.
  • Impaction: Common with wisdom teeth that are stuck beneath the gumline.
  • Orthodontics: To create space for a crowded mouth before starting braces or Invisalign.
  • Fracture: A crack that extends deep beneath the gumline or into the root.

Simple vs. Surgical Extractions

We categorize extractions into two main types based on the complexity of the case:

  • Simple Extraction: Performed on a tooth that is visible in the mouth. We use a tool called an elevator to loosen the tooth and forceps to remove it.
  • Surgical Extraction: Required if the tooth has broken off at the gumline or has not erupted yet. This involves a small incision in the gum and, occasionally, removing a small amount of bone to access the root.

The Removal of Wisdom Teeth

Wisdom teeth, or "third molars," are the most commonly extracted teeth. Because they often lack enough room to emerge properly, they can become impacted, leading to pain, cysts, or damage to neighboring healthy teeth. Early evaluation in the late teens is recommended to plan for removal before roots are fully formed.

What to Expect During the Procedure

Modern dentistry makes extractions virtually painless. The process typically follows these steps:

  1. Local Anesthesia: We thoroughly numb the tooth and the surrounding gum tissue. You will feel pressure, but no sharp pain.
  2. Sedation (Optional): For anxious patients, we offer "laughing gas" or oral sedation to ensure a calm experience.
  3. The Extraction: The tooth is gently rocked to expand the socket and removed.
  4. Hemostasis: Gauze is applied to the site to encourage the formation of a blood clot, which is vital for healing.

Critical Aftercare: Preventing Dry Socket

Proper home care is essential to avoid Dry Socket—a painful condition where the blood clot dislodges. Follow these rules for the first 24–48 hours:

  • No Straws: The suction can pull the clot out of the socket.
  • No Smoking: Chemicals and suction interfere with healing.
  • Soft Foods: Stick to yogurt, lukewarm soup, and smoothies (eaten with a spoon).
  • Gentle Rinsing: Do not spit forcefully; let the water fall out of your mouth.

Bone Grafting & Socket Preservation

When a tooth is removed, the surrounding jawbone can begin to shrink. If you plan on getting a dental implant in the future, we may recommend a Bone Graft (Socket Preservation) at the time of extraction. This involves placing bone material into the empty socket to maintain its volume and density.

What Happens After Extraction?

Unless it is a wisdom tooth, an extracted tooth should be replaced to prevent surrounding teeth from shifting. We will discuss permanent solutions with you, including:

  • Dental Implants: The gold standard for a natural look and feel.
  • Dental Bridges: Fixed restorations that "bridge" the gap.
  • Partial Dentures: Removable options for replacing multiple teeth.
